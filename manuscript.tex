\begin{abstract}
Digital twins are becoming a reference architecture for modern power grids, with machine
learning inside them. We ask whether literature naming a grid digital twin and a
security term engages AI-enabled threats---and the twin itself---as an attack target, or
stops at defensive use. The hypothesis came from early reading of this corpus; in its
original form---defensive use common, attack-side engagement rare---it was not supported,
and the framing was revised post hoc to a question of depth.
Twelve OpenAlex queries yielded 1{,}287 records (492 eligible); a declared
non-probabilistic ranking retained 100, 83 coded at full text across 46 fields.
Every figure describes this corpus, not the wider literature.
Machine learning is used in 70 of 83---detection in 55; 35 (42\%) engage an AI-enabled
threat at any level, 11 (13\%) substantively; in the 29-study security-primary
detection-ML core, 16 (55\%) engage any, 6 (21\%) substantively. Retrieval ran on
defensive vocabulary, so these rates describe the defensively-framed literature only. The
defensible conclusion: any-level engagement is common, depth is not reliably
measured here---``any'' can mean under two sentences, the substantive tier sits on the
weakest coder agreement, and its threshold partly proxies document length.
As coded, no grid study treats the twin itself as the adversary's objective; two read
nuclear-domain records (R91, R92) attack twin interfaces and ML models, bounding it
from outside; 56 of 83 name classical threats on the twin's data path---surfaces to
protect, not studied targets. A five-item agenda ties each item to its gap, disconfirming
evidence, and dual-use obligations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Electric power grids are digitising faster than they are being secured. Wide-area measurement,
distributed energy resources, electric-vehicle charging and market-coupled control have turned
the grid into a cyber-physical system whose behaviour is increasingly mediated by software, and
real incidents---the 2015 and 2016 attacks on the Ukrainian distribution grid among
them---have shown what software-mediated disruption costs \cite{eisac2016,liang2017}. The
digital twin---a synchronised, bidirectional virtual counterpart of a physical asset, updated
from live sensor streams---has moved in the same decade from concept to a reference
architecture for monitoring, protection and control, in the grid as elsewhere
\cite{glaessgen2012,grieves2014,tao2019}. That
same synchronisation is what makes the twin valuable and what makes it a target.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has followed the twin into the grid. Machine learning now performs
state estimation, anomaly detection, load and attack forecasting, and increasingly control.
The security community has been quick to adopt it. The question this review
asks is whether the same community has been as quick to model AI---and the twin itself---as
something an adversary can attack. Throughout, `digital twin' must be named by the authors, so
adversarial-ML work on grid security that does not mention a twin is out of scope and is not
represented in any figure here.\footnote{Citation scheme: the 100 corpus records are cited
in the running text as R$<$ID$>$ (R18 = Zaidi \& Khan), listed in Appendix~A with years,
venues, venue types and citation counts, and released as bibliography keys
\texttt{ref$<$ID$>$} in the reference list. Bracketed numeric citations [n] refer
exclusively to non-corpus background literature.}

Early reading suggested a specific, testable asymmetry: grid digital-twin security research
uses AI as a defensive tool far more often than it engages AI, or the twin, as an attack
target. The hypothesis was formed from part of the same corpus, before coding began and
with no pre-specified confirmation threshold; the analysis is therefore exploratory. And
stated plainly: in its original form---defensive use common, attack-side engagement
rare---the hypothesis was \emph{not supported}. Any-level engagement is majority in the
core subsets (Section~\ref{sec:ai-threats}); what is minority is substantive engagement.
The framing was revised after seeing the data to a question of depth; the title names the
measurement actually delivered rather than the asymmetry originally hypothesised; and
every figure below should be read with that post-hoc revision in mind. Two further
limits on what the measurement can show should be stated at the outset. First, ``uses
ML for detection'' and ``engages an AI-enabled threat'' are different constructs, so
there is no normative baseline for what the engagement share \emph{should} be; the
measurement is descriptive, and we make no claim that a particular share would be
adequate or deficient. Second, the claim the measurement supports is one of
\emph{emphasis and depth}, not of \emph{rate}: we make no temporal ``adopts faster''
claim, because the corpus is a ranked, non-random subset in which a year-wise
comparison is confounded by selection (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
This review quantifies what the corpus does engage, and turns the measured gaps into a
research agenda. It is an exploratory structured review, not an empirical deployment
study.

\subsection{Positioning against prior reviews}\label{sec:positioning}

The closest prior works are Habeeb, Weli and Almaliki's 2026 PRISMA review of ``Dual-Role
Digital Twins'' in cyber-physical security \cite{habeeb2026} and, appearing concurrently
and independently of this review, Ogiesoba-Eguakun and Rath's systematic review of
cyber-resilient digital-twin architectures for smart grids \cite{ogiesoba2026};
Table~\ref{tab:priorreviews} positions this review against both and against the wider
survey literature. Habeeb et al.'s contribution is real and prior: they name the dual role---the twin as
both an expanded attack surface and a defence enabler---and set out four research directions
around it. Their corpus is 20 studies, gathered with a query that has no specific attack operator,
and spread across manufacturing, healthcare, IIoT, smart city and water management, with the
power grid represented in roughly one study.

We therefore do not claim to have discovered the dual-role tension. Our position is narrower and
complementary. Habeeb et al.\ name two axes; we measure a narrow, grid-side instance of the
attack-surface axis---the twin as the adversary's explicit objective---with a published
corpus, a closed threat vocabulary, and reported coder reliability. We additionally report
looser tiers (the twin as an attacked data path or model, and the twin as a protected asset),
because the strict-objective tier alone cannot speak to the broader attack-surface axis.
In other words, they build the theory; we report a lower bound on its narrowest grid
manifestation, not a test of the full axis. Section~\ref{sec:related} sets out the mapping in full, including the five attack vectors their
review identifies and our corpus's coverage of them, and compares this review's design with
the wider family of prior surveys (Table~\ref{tab:priorreviews}).

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A screened corpus of 100 studies on digital twins for power-grid cybersecurity,
  drawn from 1{,}287 unique records of twelve OpenAlex core queries by a \emph{declared,

  \item[RQ2] Which threats are named, at which grid domains and lifecycle stages, and with what

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Digital twins in power systems}

A digital twin \cite{grieves2014,glaessgen2012,tao2019} is a virtual representation of a
physical entity that is connected to it by a
bidirectional data link, such that the virtual state tracks the physical state and, in the
stronger formulations, the physical entity can be actuated through the twin. Three properties
distinguish a twin from an ordinary model or simulation: synchronisation with live data,
bidirectionality, and asset specificity. Grid applications span state estimation and
observability, protection and control, fault and attack diagnosis, asset-health monitoring, and
training and planning (R16, R24, R50).

The maturity of grid twins varies widely. Some reported twins are conceptual architectures with
no implementation; many are prototypes demonstrated on simulation or testbed data; a small
number report validation against real grid assets (R54, R74). This maturity gradient
matters for security, because a threat model that is plausible on a conceptual twin may not be
reachable on a real deployment, and vice versa.

\subsection{Cyber threats to grids and to twins}

The classical grid threat model is adversarial and studied: false data injection against state
estimation \cite{liu2011,liang2017}, replay, denial of service, man-in-the-middle on telemetry
links, false command injection, and supply-chain compromise. These are conventional
cyber-physical attacks, are well represented in the corpus (R1, R3), and
need no AI to execute (Section~\ref{sec:threats}).

The twinning layer adds attack surface that the classical model does not cover. The
bidirectional link can be exploited in both directions; the twin synchronises with sensor
streams, so any data-poisoning channel into those streams reaches the twin; and where the twin
uses learned components---detectors, forecasters, controllers---those components can themselves
be attacked by distribution-shift, evasion, or training-time poisoning. This is the attack-surface
axis that Habeeb et al.\ name \cite{habeeb2026}.

\subsection{AI on both sides of the boundary}

AI enters grid security from two directions. Defensively, machine learning performs intrusion
and anomaly detection, attack classification, load and attack forecasting, and increasingly
control. Offensively, the same model families are themselves vulnerable: adversarial examples
can evade learned detectors \cite{szegedy2014,goodfellow2015}, poisoning can corrupt training
data drawn from compromised sensor streams \cite{biggio2018}, and large language models
introduce prompt-level and social-engineering vectors that
did not exist in earlier grids. An ``AI-enabled threat'' in this review means an attack in which
the adversary uses AI, or attacks an AI component, or both---as distinct from a conventional
cyber-physical attack that happens to be detected by an AI method. These two directions have
different implications (an AI-wielded attack changes the adversary; an attack on an AI
component changes the victim), and the headline construct \emph{combines} them. The coding
does not separate the two directions per record; Section~\ref{sec:ai-threats} reports a
notes-based separation for the substantive studies and flags the lumping as a construct
limitation (Section~\ref{sec:validity}).

A brief terminology note. `Digital twin' has largely superseded `CPS model' and `HIL simulation'
in recent grids literature, so papers that functionally twin a grid without using the phrase are
out of scope of the search, despite carrying relevant threat models.

\section{Related work and positioning}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{The closest review}

Habeeb, Weli and Almaliki (2026, \emph{Digital Twin}) review Dual-Role Digital Twins for
cyber-physical security with PRISMA 2020: 412 identified, 318 screened, 72 eligibility-checked,
20 included, spanning January 2019 to December 2025 \cite{habeeb2026}. They ground the dual role
in Complex Adaptive Systems, functional duality, and zero-trust architecture, and they name five
attack vectors. Their search string is
\texttt{("Digital Twin" OR "DT") AND}
\texttt{("Cybersecurity" OR "Attack Surface" OR}
\texttt{"Defense" OR "Honeypot" OR "Intrusion Detection")}. It contains no term for a specific attack (no adversarial
machine learning, poisoning, evasion, or reverse-pathway operator), so attack-side records have
to enter through the security and defence terms. The same limitation applies to our twelve core
queries, which is why we added the four adversarial-AI probes.

\subsection{The bridge: their vectors, our vocabulary}

Our codebook's threat vocabulary was designed before we read their review, according to
the dated codebook and design log in the repository---a claim that cannot be verified
from this text alone. The review's \emph{positioning}, by contrast, was developed in
engagement with their work (Section~\ref{sec:positioning}): the framing took its
final shape against theirs, the vocabulary did not change. We state both facts
separately and make no stronger independence claim than that. A vocabulary-level mapping from our threat terms onto their five attack
vectors exists---every one of their vectors is expressible in our closed vocabulary,
and our vocabulary's LLM-specific (4 records) and agentic (2) constructs fall outside
their five vectors altogether---but the mapping is a single-author judgment that was
not double-coded, so it is released as a repository artifact
(\texttt{VECTOR\_MAPPING.md} in the repository's \texttt{analysis/} directory) and is
not presented in this paper as evidence
that any vector is dealt with empirically.

The relationship between the two search designs is the mirror image of the mapping. Our
search adds retrieval operators their query does not contain---adversarial machine learning,
poisoning, evasion and large-language-model terms---so records whose visibility depends on
those terms could enter our pool but not theirs; the added terms that do not constitute
novel constructs map onto their own vectors, which is why the mapping claims coverage
rather than novelty, and why the classical \texttt{dos} and \texttt{fdi} terms are placed
there under the vector whose surface they exploit rather than by an identity of mechanism.

\subsection{Other reviews in the corpus}

Several corpus entries are themselves reviews (R1, R3, R16, R24, R50, R57). Their
coverage is broad on the defensive side. Our contribution relative to them is the quantified
attack-side measurement: we do not merely note that AI threats matter, we count how often the
corpus engages them and find that any-level engagement is common---majority in the core
subsets---while substantive engagement is the minority case (11 of 83 full-text records).
The finding is one of depth, not of presence versus absence.

\subsection{Comparison with prior reviews of digital-twin security}\label{sec:priorcompare}

Three further families of prior reviews bear on this review's question, and
Table~\ref{tab:priorreviews} states the design comparison across all of them. General
surveys of digital-twin security \cite{alcaraz2022,mun2025} map the threat landscape
and countermeasures for the twin paradigm across cyber-physical domains: Alcaraz and
Lopez classify threats to the twinning layer and the countermeasures against them for
Industry 4.0 systems, and Mun et al.\ extend the threat-and-requirements mapping with
security requirements and trust dimensions. These surveys describe what threatens a
twin and how to defend one; they do not measure how often the studies of any particular
sector engage each threat. A second family treats the twin as the security
\emph{instrument}: El-Hajj et al.\ \cite{elhajj2024} systematically review digital twins
used to enhance Industry 4.0 security and classify that literature by sector, twin
purpose and enabling technology---the defensive half of the dual role. A third family
surveys energy-sector twins with security as one topic among several: Heluany and
Gkioulos \cite{heluany2024} review 81 studies of digital twins for power generation and
distribution and observe that security research ``often presents the digital twin as an
additional layer of protection, yet rarely investigates the security of the digital twin
by itself''; Barreto and Aoki \cite{barreto2025} review 74 publications on power-system
twins, with cybersecurity one of seven application areas. The two closest reviews are
the dual-role review \cite{habeeb2026} discussed above and in
Section~\ref{sec:positioning}, and the concurrent, independent systematic review of
cyber-resilient twin architectures for smart grids by Ogiesoba-Eguakun and Rath
\cite{ogiesoba2026}, which synthesises attack modelling, detection, mitigation and
secure control across 149 publications.

What no review in Table~\ref{tab:priorreviews} reports is a per-study measurement of
attack-side engagement: whether each underlying study treats the twin or its learned
components as an attack object, at what depth, against which threats, and how reliably
two readers would agree on the answer. Nor do they attach agreement statistics to
their own syntheses (Heluany and Gkioulos cross-check 10\% of included and 5\% of
excluded papers, without agreement coefficients), condition their conclusions on their
retrieval designs, or carry uncertainty intervals on their coding instruments. This
review supplies exactly that measurement layer at grid scale: a per-study stance
coding, a tiered twin-role measurement, a 0--2 depth-of-engagement scale per AI-threat
vector, field-by-field reliability with misclassification propagation to the headline,
a strict-scope and venue-filter sensitivity battery, and a research agenda whose items
each carry disconfirming evidence. The comparison in the table is deliberately one of
design scope and measurement layer, not of merit: each prior review does work this
review does not attempt, and the facts the comparison rests on---query designs, corpus
sizes, the presence or absence of engagement quantification and reliability
reporting---are stated in the cited works themselves. Corpus sizes are additionally not
directly comparable across review designs (a PRISMA-screened included set, a
ProKnow-C portfolio and a declared ranked subset answer different questions); the
Habeeb et al.\ row rests on a full-text reading of that review, the other rows on the
published records as cited.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Design}

This is a \emph{structured} literature review reported in PRISMA-2020 flow format for
transparency of search and selection. It is not a systematic review in the PRISMA sense:
it searches one database (OpenAlex), has no registered protocol, and retains a ranked,
non-random subset of the eligible records. We use PRISMA flow reporting because the
alternatives (a narrative review) would hide the selection rule rather than expose it.
The protocol was fixed before extraction, with the codebook revisions noted below. The
review is theory-testing in intent: the asymmetry stated in the introduction is the
hypothesis, and the retained 100 studies are the corpus on which it is explored---with no
pre-specified confirmation threshold, as the introduction states.

\subsection{Search strategy}

We searched OpenAlex with twelve core queries combining a digital-twin term, a grid-domain term,
and a security term, yielding 1{,}287 unique records after de-duplication. We then ran four
additional adversarial-AI probes---combining digital-twin and grid terms with adversarial
machine learning, poisoning, evasion, and large-language-model terms---which raised the
candidate pool to approximately 2{,}000. The probes exist because the core grid-security
vocabulary is not expected to retrieve the attack-side literature.

What the released record does \emph{not} establish is the probes' effect on the corpus. The
screened set of 1{,}287 records derives from the twelve core queries; per-record query
provenance for the probe-added records (roughly 700) was not retained, so we cannot report
how many of the 492 eligible, or of the retained 100, are retrievable only through the
probes. The retrieval-bias defence that the probes were designed to provide therefore
cannot be made from these data, and we do not make it (Section~\ref{sec:validity}, Search
bias). Releasing the query strings, the run dates and the per-record provenance is a
precondition for resolving this. The repository carries two artifacts on this point: a provenance
manifest (`SEARCH\_PROVENANCE.md`) stating exactly what must be released, and a dated
snapshot (`analysis/search\_snapshot\_2026-10-19/`) containing \emph{reconstructed}
query strings (the originals were not preserved) together with a second-database
(Crossref) run of each string on 2026-10-19. The original strings themselves remain a
required artifact, and OpenAlex re-runs attempted twice on 2026-10-19 were blocked both
times by the API's shared-IP budget limit. What
this means for the results is stated here rather than only in
Section~\ref{sec:validity}: the corpus derives from defensive-vocabulary retrieval, so
any attack-side rate measured on it---including the RQ3 headline---is conditional on
that retrieval design and says nothing about the attack-side literature the queries do
not retrieve (Section~\ref{sec:validity}, Search bias).

\subsection{Selection and inclusion}

We screened the 1{,}287 core-query records on title and abstract. A record was eligible if
its title or abstract contained at least one digital-twin term, at least one grid, power or
energy term, and at least one cyber or security term; 795 records failed at least one of the
three conditions, leaving 492 eligible.

The grid term is deliberately broad---\emph{grid, power or energy}---and the eligibility
rule therefore admits adjacent domains. Four retained records are outside the power-grid
domain on a strict reading (a vehicle-twin threat-modelling review, a cyber-fraud study, a
network-traffic anomaly-detection paper with no grid or twin instantiation, and a nuclear
physical-protection risk analysis in which no twin is instantiated); three more are
borderline (nuclear digital twins, battery twins for telecom supply, and a security-education
cyber twin). A strict-scope sensitivity analysis that removes these records is reported
alongside every headline figure (Section~\ref{sec:ai-threats}); the borderline records are
listed there with their identifiers.

The eligible set was then narrowed to 100 by a non-probabilistic rule combining OpenAlex
relevance score, citation count, and recency, with the final cut at rank 100. This ranking
is a \emph{selection} step, not an eligibility criterion. Its components pull in opposite
directions---citations favour older, established work; recency favours new work---but
which component dominated the realised cut cannot be established from the released
record, and the corpus's ID ordering does not speak to it. The IDs are best read as a
display order: year descending, and within each year block citations descending (IDs
1--45 are all 2026; per-record citation counts are given in the Cit.\ column of
Appendix~A). That pattern is compatible with a composite rule followed by this sort,
and with a purely year-first rule only if the 492 eligible records contained at most 76
records from 2025--26, which the released record cannot check; the ID ordering therefore
carries no information about the selection rule. The clean test is the year distribution
of the 492 eligible records against the retained 100, which would show directly which
years the cut discarded. The 492-record export was not preserved; the provenance
manifest lists its release as a required artifact, so the test cannot be run on the
released record (Section~\ref{sec:validity}, Search bias). What the released record does
establish is the realised corpus's profile: 40 of the 100 retained records have zero
citations at retrieval, 76\% of the corpus is from 2025--26, and 24 of the 100 are from
2020--2024. The corpus is therefore recency-heavy; we report that profile and make no
claim about which component produced it. The profile does favour reviews (36\% of the
retained corpus). Because the retained subset is not a random sample, every prevalence
figure in Section~\ref{sec:results} is a statement about the retained 100 studies and
should not be read as an estimate of the wider literature; the corpus's 76\% share of
2025--26 records is partly an artefact of the selection, not a finding about the field.
Small denominators bound all percentages, and the uncertainty that a confidence interval
should carry here is coding misclassification, which we quantify in Section~\ref{sec:agreement}
rather than with sampling intervals.

\subsection{Screening}

Screening was title-and-abstract, applying the three-term eligibility rule above: 795 of
the 1{,}287 core-query records were excluded for missing at least one of the three terms
(digital twin; grid, power or energy; cyber or security), leaving 492 eligible records.
Inclusion then ranked the eligible set by relevance, citation count, and recency, and
retained the top 100, as declared in the selection step above. Figure~\ref{fig:prisma} is

\subsection{Extraction}

We extracted 46 fields per record. Extraction was computer-assisted but human-coded: a script
surfaced keyword hits and context snippets for each field, and every code was assigned by a
human reading those snippets in context. Keyword snippets were scored against the full text and
the surrounding paragraph, not the excerpt alone; reference lists and author biographies were
stripped before scanning, so absence claims rest on the surviving body text. The extraction is
reproducible from the coded excerpts and DOIs, which are released.

Of the 100 records, 83 were available in full text and coded in full; 17 were available only as
abstracts and were coded at abstract level only. All abstract-only fields that require full text are treated as
missing, not as zero, throughout. The 17\% non-retrieval rate is an access limitation of the
authors (paywalled or blocked full texts), not a property of the literature or a reproducibility
finding; the affected records are listed with DOIs so readers can see exactly what is missing
(Appendix~A). The 17 are not a random subset of the corpus, and the loss is not neutral for
the headline: an abstract-level reading of the 17 (title plus abstract) codes 15 as \emph{tool}
and the two exceptions---R91 and R92, Spirito et al., OSTI/Idaho National Laboratory reports
on nuclear reactors---as attack-side work. Both were obtained and read:
R91 in full text (48 pages, retrieved from the INL digital library; the OSTI full-text
endpoint was unreachable from our environment) and R92 from its extended report abstract
on OSTI (two full-text retrieval attempts failed). R91 analyses attack scenarios against
the twin itself---an SSRF exploit of a cloud twin instance used to exfiltrate the twin
and replicate it offline in simulation mode, and data deception of the twin's autonomous
control (``by modifying the data received by the digital twin, it was deceived into
throttling the output from the power supplies'')---while R92 constructs multi-rate
hyperparameter attacks on the twin's ML models, with an event-triggered
predictive-control response demonstrated in simulation. A post-hoc, single-author
reading codes both as \emph{both}-role records (attack side and countermeasures; the
per-record coding is released as \texttt{READBACK.md} in the repository's
\texttt{analysis/} directory); neither is merged
into the frozen matrix, and neither is a grid-domain record. The strict-target null
below therefore carries its ``grid-domain'' qualifier on read evidence, not only on
abstract labels: the corpus's own nuclear-domain records demonstrate twin-directed
attack work (Section~\ref{sec:stance}).

\subsection{Codebook}

The codebook defines each field and its controlled vocabulary, and was revised five times during
extraction. The revisions are logged in the repository and are load-bearing:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{R2} distinguishes a review from an enabling paper, and classifies
  attack-generating work as \emph{bridge}.
  \item \textbf{R3} requires a named interface or protocol before \texttt{attack\_surface\_named}
  may be coded \emph{yes}; passing mentions do not qualify.
  \item \textbf{R4} prevents passing mentions from triggering the \emph{multi} lifecycle value.
  \item \textbf{R5} contracts \texttt{ml\_family} to a controlled vocabulary.
  \item \textbf{R6} fixes evaluation-method precedence (\textit{hardware-in-the-loop} $>$
  testbed $>$ simulation) and maps surveys to \emph{concept} maturity.
\end{itemize}

The \texttt{twin\_role} field requires positive evidence. \emph{both} needs explicit coverage of
the defensive and attack-target roles \emph{and} of the tension between them; \emph{target}
requires that the twin be the adversary's objective rather than an asset to be protected.
\emph{both} $= 0$ is therefore definitional: a paper cannot score \emph{both} without stating
the tension, and we report that zero as a statement about \emph{explicit articulation}, not
as evidence that dual-role practice is absent. The category is attainable---the two
nuclear-domain records (R91, R92) both read as \emph{both}
(Section~\ref{sec:method} on extraction)---so the zero among the 83 grid-frame full texts
is a statement about this corpus, not about the category. The strict \emph{target} rule is likewise one
end of a tiered measurement: in a security setting an asset that needs protecting is an
asset an adversary may attack, so Section~\ref{sec:stance} reports three tiers side by
side---twin as the adversary's objective (strict), twin's data path or learned components
named as an attack surface (interface and model threats from the closed vocabulary), and
twin as protected asset (\emph{tool}). Candidates such as R84, ``Enhancing the
cybersecurity of battery digital twins,'' were read as \emph{tool} because the twin remains
an asset under protection; under the looser tiers that study counts as naming an interface
threat, which is the reading its content supports.

\subsection{Scoring of AI-threat engagement}

Each AI-enabled threat field was coded on a 0--2 ordinal scale: 0 = not addressed; 1 =
mentioned, listed, or peripheral (fewer than two sentences or no failure-mode detail);
2 = substantive (a threat model, mechanism, experiment, or mitigation specific to that
vector, or a section dedicated to it). The threshold was applied to the full text rather than
the abstract. The threshold also makes the substantive tier partly a proxy for document
length: a conference-length paper structurally has less room than a journal article or a
thesis to clear ``a section dedicated to it'', so depth comparisons across venue formats
conflate engagement with length. The corpus shows the expected signature---substantive
engagement in 9 of 62 journal articles, 1 of 4 preprints and 1 of 1 thesis, but 0 of 14
conference papers (Section~\ref{sec:ai-threats})---and the depth claim is qualified
accordingly wherever it is made. \texttt{ai\_threat\_overall} is 1 if any vector is 1 or 2, and 2 if any vector
is 2. Both coders coded the scale independently; per-vector agreement is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:agreement}.

\subsection{Reliability}\label{sec:reliability}

A second coder independently coded 20 of the 83 full-text records (24\% of the full-text
corpus, drawn from the full-text frame only), blind to coder 1's values and to the screening
label. Both coders are authors of this review; coder 1 is the codebook author and coded 80\%
of the corpus. The sample of 20 contains about 0.2 expected \emph{target} cases at corpus
prevalence, so \texttt{twin\_role} agreement carries almost no information about the rare
class that the headline needs; the same holds for the rarer AI vectors. Agreement is reported
field by field in Section~\ref{sec:agreement} as Cohen's $\kappa$ with bootstrap 95\%
intervals, PABAK (prevalence-adjusted bias-adjusted kappa, which removes the prevalence
depression that affects $\kappa$), and a bias index (the largest between-coder difference in
category marginals), together with percentage agreement where a field is near-constant and
$\kappa$ is undefined. With $n = 20$, the large-sample standard error of $\kappa$ is of the
order of 0.2--0.3 even for the better fields; cells in Table~\ref{tab:reliability} are
point estimates, and a stable ranking of field difficulty is not achievable from this
sample.

\subsection{Adjudication}

All field-level disagreements were adjudicated against the codebook and the underlying evidence.
The rules, stated here rather than buried, are: adopt the value the codebook and evidence
dictate; where both coders' values are defensible, coder 1's prevails; where neither fits, the
codebook rule decides. Adjudication changed 20 net cells (Section~\ref{sec:agreement}). No
headline field moved, but under a tie-break that defaults to coder 1 this is the expected
outcome, not evidence of robustness: headline fields could only have moved had the
adjudicator overridden coder 1, and the adjudicator works with coder 1. Adjudication
therefore does not test the headline; the released pre-adjudication codes are the honest
artefact, and they are published frozen alongside the adjudicated matrix.

\subsection{False-negative audit}

Because the headline is an absence---of attack-side engagement---we audited it, and we
report the audit with its design limits stated rather than implied. We ran a strict
AI-threat lexicon over the 48 records coded as \emph{not engaging} any AI threat and a
broader eleven-family net. Both the extraction and the audit are keyword-assisted, so the
audit shares the extraction's blind spot: ``no engagement'' partly means ``no keyword
hit'', and asking a lexicon to re-find what a lexicon missed is close to circular. Its
positive control recovers only 7 of the 11 substantive positives, and its recall on
mentioned-only (score-1) engagement---the harder case, covering 24 of the 35 engaged
records---was never measured. Of the 48 negatives, 38 fired the broad net and 23 fired a
strong family; the 23 strong firings were individually inspected at audit time, and the
15 weak-only firings were inspected by an author reading the released audit windows and
stored abstracts (post hoc, single-reader; Section~\ref{sec:audit}).
A seeded 12-record sample of the 48 negatives was re-read the same way. The audit is
retained as a disclosure of the false-negative channel, not as a bound on it; the design
that would bound it---an independent full-text re-read of a random sample of the 48
negatives by a coder outside the author team---is stated as required future work in
Section~\ref{sec:audit}.

\subsection{Verification and reproducibility}

Every DOI was verified against an authoritative registry: 96 of 97 resolved with a
title match (95 exact, one near), one DOI is not registered, and three corpus records carry no
DOI. Corpus metadata was cross-checked against Crossref, and 18 of 54 venue labels disagreed,
which we resolved in favour of the registry. All extraction data, the codebook, the coding
sheets, the reliability script, and the figure script are released as a reproducibility package.

\subsection{How the twin is positioned (RQ1)}
\label{sec:stance}

Across all 100 records, the adjudicated stance distribution is defensive 42, review 36, bridge
10, enabling 10, adversarial 2; security is the primary subject in 83 of 100
(Figure~\ref{fig:stance}a). The distribution differs from the screening seed---which had
adversarial 11, review 23---confirming that keyword-level stance labelling is unreliable and that
reading is necessary (Figure~\ref{fig:stance}b).

The sharper result is \texttt{twin\_role}, coded on the 83 full-text records: the twin is treated
as a defensive \emph{tool} in 75---a near-constant default (90\%) on which coder agreement
therefore carries almost no information about the rare classes---as an attack \emph{target}
in exactly 1, as \emph{both} in 0,
and is \emph{unclear} in 7. The single strict \emph{target} record (R59) is a
vehicle-twin threat-modelling review, outside the grid domain; under a strict grid scope the
target count is zero. The \emph{both} $=0$ result is definitional (the codebook requires
explicit articulation of the tension), so it records the absence of \emph{explicit
articulation} of the dual role that Habeeb et al.\ name \cite{habeeb2026}, not the absence of
dual-role practice.

Because ``asset under protection'' and ``adversary's objective'' are not separable in a
security setting, Table~\ref{tab:twintiers} reports the tiered measurement side by side.
Beyond the single strict-target record, 56 of 83 full-text studies name an interface
threat on the twin's data path (man-in-the-middle 26, replay 23, reverse pathway 10,
false command injection 22; interface threats combined 44) or a model threat on its
learned components (poisoning, evasion, prompt-level; 27, overlapping the interface set
in 15 records). Of the 75 \emph{tool} records, 51 name such a threat: the dominant
pattern in the corpus is a twin defended against threats whose target surface includes
the twin itself, without the twin being treated as the adversary's objective. The strict
rule is not blind to contrary language: the corpus contains one-sentence assertions that
the twin ``is a main target for hackers'' (R48) and that a compromised twin could cause
``unauthorized data access, manipulated simulations'' (R88); both were read and set aside
as undeveloped, non-AI statements, which is the strict rule working as designed rather
than evidence suppression. Two qualifications keep the middle tier honest.
First, the interface threats are classical grid-communication attacks that any twin-based
defender would name; the tier measures defender-framed threat-\emph{naming} that bears on
the twin's data path, not that the twin's own surface is the analytical object of the
study, and the tier label is chosen accordingly. Second, the most direct field for this
claim, \texttt{attack\_surface\_named}, has prevalence 55/83 (66.3\%) and
the lowest agreement in the reliability study on both statistics ($\kappa = 0.14$, PABAK
0.20, AC1 0.31; Section~\ref{sec:agreement}), so the tier boundaries carry that
uncertainty.

\subsection{Engagement with AI-enabled threats (RQ3)}

\label{sec:ai-threats}
Note on the two threat field families, unified here for the rest of the paper: the closed
classical vocabulary (\texttt{threat\_models\_named}) and the AI-engagement fields
(\texttt{ai\_*}) overlap in vocabulary but differ in construct---naming a threat versus
engaging it on the 0--2 scale. The overlap is near-nested in the data: of the 22 records
naming \texttt{poisoning}, 19 are coded \texttt{ai\_poisoning} $\ge 1$; of the 11 naming
\texttt{evasion}, 9 are coded \texttt{ai\_evasion} $\ge 1$; \texttt{prompt} is named
once while \texttt{ai\_llm} $\ge 1$ in 4 records. The \texttt{ai\_*} fields are the
operative measurement for AI-threat engagement; the classical vocabulary's AI-adjacent
terms are retained only for family-level reading (Section~\ref{sec:agreement}), and the
residual named-but-not-engaged records (poisoning: R64, R68, R87; evasion: R15, R51) are
classical usages---ARP poisoning, the SolarWinds incident as a data-poisoning example,
evasion of non-ML supervisors.
This is the headline, reported with matched denominators. Machine learning is used in 70 of
the 83 full-text studies; its dominant role is detection (55), followed by forecasting (12),
none (13), and control (3). Forecasting and control are not security functions, so the
security-relevant defensive figure is 55/83 (66\%), not 70/83. Across the same 83 records,
35 (42.2\%) engage at least one AI-enabled threat at any level, 11 (13.3\%) engage one
substantively, and 48 (57.8\%) do not engage at all (Figure~\ref{fig:aithreats}). All 35
engaged studies are among the 70 that use machine learning at all, and 30 of the 55
(55\%) detection-ML studies engage an AI threat---a majority---so the non-engagement among
ML-using studies is 35 of 70 (50\%) rather than a zero/one split. The defensible reading
of these numbers is the one the instrument supports: \emph{any-level engagement is common
in this corpus, and depth is not reliably measured here}. Three reasons, stated once here
and detailed in Sections~\ref{sec:agreement} and~\ref{sec:validity}. (i) ``Any'' can
mean fewer than two sentences. (ii) The substantive tier is coded on the weakest collapse
in the study ($\kappa = 0.14$, with only 1 of 6 positives agreed by both coders).
(iii) The substantive threshold is partly a document-length proxy---0 of 14 conference
papers clear it, against 9 of 62 journal articles, 1 of 4 preprints and 1 of 1 thesis---so
``depth'' is confounded with venue format even within the non-review set. And one
condition frames everything: the corpus was retrieved by defensive vocabulary
(Section~\ref{sec:method}), so these rates describe the defensively-framed literature,
not the attack-side literature the queries do not retrieve.

Three denominator restrictions probe it. Restricting to the 49 non-review studies: 20 of
49 (41\%) engage any, 6 of 49 substantively. Restricting to the 41 security-primary,
non-review studies: 16 of 41 (39\%) engage any, 6 of 41 substantively. Restricting to the
29 security-primary, non-review, detection-ML studies---the subset with the strongest
obligation to model attacks on their own detectors---16 of 29 (55\%) engage any AI-enabled
threat and 6 of 29 (21\%) substantively. Majority any-level engagement therefore holds on
the two core denominators only---the 29-study core subset and the 55 detection-ML studies
(16/29; 30/55)---while on every wider denominator it is minority (35/83, 42\%; 20/49,
41\%; 16/41, 39\%); substantive engagement is minority on every denominator (at or below
one fifth). Majority defensive use thus coexists with majority any-level engagement in
the core subsets and with minority substantive engagement everywhere; the honest summary
of the asymmetry is that engagement is common but shallow in the core subsets and thin
outside them, and there is no external baseline for how deep it should be. These
restrictions re-slice the same coded data (Section~\ref{sec:denominators}), so their
agreement shows stability of the shared codes, not independent robustness. The same
pattern holds under the strict grid scope inside the core subset: removing R18 and R69
leaves 27 studies, of which 15 (56\%) engage any and 5 (19\%) substantively.

Because the corpus is a ranked, non-random subset, a year-wise comparison cannot
support a rate claim; Table~\ref{tab:yearwise} reports it as description only. Within the
corpus, ML use is 85\% or better from 2024 onward (6 of 7 in 2024, 23 of 27 in 2025, 35
of 37 in 2026; 2023 is 4 of 6, 2022 is 2 of 2), and
substantive engagement in the three years with at least five records (2024--2026) never
exceeds 22.2\% (6 of 27 in 2025); the small-denominator years are not interpretable as
rates---2022 shows 1 of
2 (50\%) substantive---and the 2020--21 and 2023 cells show zero engagement on small
denominators. We draw no temporal conclusion from any of these cells.

Per vector, at any/substantive level: adversarial machine learning 24/8, data or model

The construct behind these counts combines two directions that should be reported
separately: attacks \emph{on} AI components and attacks \emph{wielded with} AI. The coding
does not separate them per record. A notes-based reading of the eleven substantive studies
classifies four as attack generation with AI (adversarial-ML or GAN synthesis of attacks;
R22, R49, R57 and R69---the last of unattributed authorship in a low-tier venue and
outside the strict grid scope, flagged again in Section~\ref{sec:validity}), five as
attacks on learned components (poisoning, evasion or prompt-level risks; R16, R17, R24,
R54, R74), and two as both (R50, R94); the any-engagement count of 35 mixes the two
directions in unknown proportions. The headline is therefore an engagement figure, not an
attack-type figure.

A strict-scope sensitivity addresses the breadth of the grid term. Excluding the four
records outside the power-grid domain on a strict reading (R18, R59, R69, R97: a
network-traffic anomaly-detection paper with no grid or twin instantiation, the
vehicle-twin review, the cyber-fraud study, and the nuclear physical-protection risk
analysis) leaves 79 full-text records: 33 (41.8\%) engage any AI-enabled threat, 10
(12.7\%) substantively, ML use 67, detection 52, and the strict twin-as-target count is
zero. Excluding additionally the three borderline records (R34, nuclear digital twins;
R84, battery twins for telecom supply; R68, a security-education cyber twin) leaves 76
records: 32 (42.1\%) any, 10 (13.2\%) substantive. Within the 29-study core subset the
same strict scope removes R18 and R69, leaving 27 studies: 15 (55.6\%) any, 5 (18.5\%)
substantive (and 25 studies, 14 any and 5 substantive, if the borderline records are also
removed). The asymmetry's direction is robust to scope; its magnitude moves by at most
about one and a half points on the any-level share.

common than substantive (score 2) for every engaged vector except LLM threats (2 mentioned,
2 substantive, a tie on a base of 4) and agentic threats (1 mentioned, 1 substantive, a tie

substantive agentic case, and one of the two substantive LLM cases sit in a single review
(R50), with the other substantive LLM case in R16 (at any level, 3 of the 4
LLM-engaged records are reviews). On the review authorship
question the substantive set has 5 reviews (45\%) against 34 (41\%) in the full-text base;
both counts are small and the difference is unsupported by any test.

Table~\ref{tab:substantive} lists the eleven substantive studies. Nine are journal
articles (all 2025--26), one is a thesis (R74) and one a preprint (R94, 2022); five carry
the \emph{review} stance and three the \emph{bridge} stance, so 8 of 11 are review-or-bridge
work.
All eleven use ML predominantly for detection. No study's primary contribution is an
AI-enabled attack on a grid digital twin; the deepest engagements are themselves reviews
(R16, R50). One of the eleven (R69) lies outside the grid domain on a strict reading, is
excluded from the strict-scope counts above, and is examined further in
Section~\ref{sec:validity}.

Two quality-bound sensitivities qualify the substantive count. Excluding R69 alone
(unattributed authorship, IRJMETS venue, outside the strict grid scope) leaves 10 of 82
substantive at the full-text denominator and 5 of 28 in the core subset; the peer-review
venue filter (Section~\ref{sec:evidence}) leaves 8 of 58. A citation-based split runs the
other way: among the 50 cited records, 18 (36\%) engage any and 5 (10\%) substantively;
among the 33 zero-citation records, 17 (52\%) and 6 (18\%). The uncited, mostly 2025--26
records engage \emph{more}, so shallow engagement is not an artefact of weak records
alone---though the zero-citation subset's higher engagement is itself consistent with the
corpus's recency- and review-heavy profile, and no available filter cleanly separates
paper quality from field behaviour (Section~\ref{sec:validity}).

Read qualitatively, the eleven cluster into four patterns. (1) Generative and adversarial modelling as
attack generation: the twin is used to synthesise adversarially consistent attacks and GAN-based
evasion by the authors themselves (R16, R24, R49, R50, R57, R94). (2) Learned intrusion
detectors stressed under distribution shift: data poisoning (R17, R74, R94), FGSM/JSMA
perturbations (R50), and GAN-driven evasion (R49, R94). (3) LLM threats treated
as architecture-level hazards---hallucination, prompt injection, biased recommendations---in the
review literature (R16, R24, R50). (4) Agentic automation represented only once, in
R50; no study constructs an adversarial RL or agentic pipeline. ``Substantive engagement'' therefore currently means
backgrounding a threat while retaining a defensive detector.

\subsection{Evidence quality (RQ4)}\label{sec:evidence}

Machine learning is used in 70 of 83 full-text studies; its dominant role is detection (55),
followed by forecasting (12), none (13), and control (3). Evaluation method splits as
simulation 33, qualitative 28, testbed 10, conceptual 5, mixed 5, and hardware-in-the-loop 2
(R55 and R62 after adjudication). Maturity is prototype in 46, concept in 34, and
validated in only 3. Baselines are reported in 41 of 83 studies and quantitative metrics in
43; both figures mix primary studies with reviews, so the non-review denominators matter and
are reported with them: baselines rise to 37 of 49 non-review studies (76\%) and metrics to
40 of 49 (82\%), against 41/83 (49\%) and 43/83 (52\%) over all full-text records.
Limitations are stated in 67. Code is released in 3 of 83 studies and data in 16. The
evidence base is, in short, prototyping-heavy and release-light. A venue-type filter
alone (the 62 journal-classified full-text records) is not a quality filter: it retains
two Zenodo deposits (R30, R32), one GSC Online Press title (R65) and one IRJMETS title
(R69). A peer-review-filtered sensitivity that removes those four venues keeps 58
records and leaves the asymmetry's direction intact: 23/58 (39.7\%) any, 8/58 (13.8\%)
substantive, 35/58 none, 55/58 tool, and the single targeted twin (R59) is the
out-of-domain vehicle-twin review. Even this filter is a proxy---venue type and publisher
are not a peer-review certification---and no indexing-based filter was applied; readers
should weight venue quality into any figure reproduced from this review. Code and data
remain low in every denominator.

Grid domains are dominated by studies that span multiple domains (\emph{multi}, 47). Where a
single domain is named: distribution 11, unclear 7, EV charging 6, microgrid 6, transmission 5,
and substation 1. Among the 29 records naming a single domain, transmission (5) trails
distribution (11) and EV charging (6); with 47 of 83 records coded \emph{multi} and the field
at moderate agreement ($\kappa = 0.44$), domain-level comparisons carry wide uncertainty, and
we state the under-representation of transmission as an observation within this corpus rather
than a claim about the literature.

\subsection{Reliability results}

\label{sec:agreement}
Table~\ref{tab:reliability} reports agreement on the 20-record sample (24\% of the 83
full-text records). On \texttt{ai\_threat\_overall}, the $3\times3$ counts are (rows coder 1, columns coder 2):
0: 9, 1, 1; 1: 1, 3, 1; 2: 1, 2, 1. The count behind the headline, 35/83, rests on the
``any engagement vs.\ none'' collapse: coder 1 has 9 of 20, coder 2 has 9 of 20, agreement
16/20 ($\kappa$ = 0.60, PABAK 0.60, bootstrap 95\% CI 0.20--0.90). The three-class
$\kappa$ is 0.41 (PABAK 0.48); these are different statistics for different questions. On
the three-level scale itself, the ordinal statistics are
linear weighted $\kappa = 0.43$ (bootstrap 95\% CI 0.08--0.74), quadratic weighted
$\kappa = 0.44$ (0.03--0.80) and Gwet's AC1 $= 0.50$ (0.18--0.80): chance-corrected
agreement on the ordinal scale is moderate under every statistic we computed, and the
intervals are wide. The positive classes are thin: each coder labels only about 9 of 20
records as engaged and 3--4 as substantive, so at $n=20$ neither $\kappa$ nor AC1 on the
substantive class can be stable. Collapsing to ``substantive vs. not'': agreement 15/20, but
$\kappa = 0.14$ (PABAK 0.50, bootstrap 95\% CI $-0.23$ to $0.64$), because only 1 of the 6
studies either coder labelled substantive is labelled so by both (the single (2,2) cell).
The two collapses are not comparably reliable.

The disagreement on ``any engagement'' is symmetric (2 each way), but four disagreements
cannot distinguish coder bias from noise, and symmetry is not evidence of accuracy: the
interval on the difference includes under- and over-counting of the same magnitude. The
headline supported by the reliability data is the 35/83 any, not the 11/83 substantive,
which is fragile. This reading is post hoc---formed after seeing the $3\times3$ table---and
is flagged as such.

To quantify what the misclassification does to the headline, we propagate the sample error
matrix to the corpus under the assumption that coder 2's coding is the gold standard: the
coder-1-engaged records stay engaged with probability $7/9$, and the coder-1-zero records
flip to engaged with probability $2/11$, giving an expected $35 \times 7/9 + 48 \times
2/11 \approx 36$ of 83 (43.3\%; bootstrap 95\% CI 21--51). The anchoring is a choice,
and the choice is circular: coder 2 is a co-author of this review, so treating coder 2
as gold cannot validate the coding, and anchoring on coder 1 instead would reproduce
35/83 by construction. The interval 21--51 spans minority and majority readings of the
any-level share, so it is best read not as a confidence statement but as a demonstration
that the observed error structure is compatible with both---which is the honest bound on
this instrument.

Fields with substantial
agreement include \texttt{baseline\_compared} ($\kappa = 0.90$), \texttt{twin\_role} (0.77),
\texttt{ai\_poisoning} (0.73), \texttt{threat\_model\_explicit} (0.69) and
\texttt{metrics\_reported} (0.68)---though \texttt{twin\_role}'s $\kappa$ carries a bootstrap
interval of 0.00--1.00 and the sample contains about 0.2 expected \emph{target} cases, so it
says almost nothing about the rare class the headline needs, and its dominant category is a
near-constant default (75 of 83). Fields at moderate or fair
agreement include the ones the
headline most needs: \texttt{ai\_threat\_overall} (0.41), \texttt{ai\_evasion} (0.39), and the
threat-vocabulary fields, which are the least reliable data in the review at the exact-set
level (agreement 5\%). Re-reported at the family level---interface threats (\texttt{mitm},
\texttt{replay}, \texttt{reverse\_pathway}, \texttt{false\_command}), learned-model threats
(\texttt{poisoning}, \texttt{evasion}, \texttt{prompt}), and classical threats
(\texttt{dos}, \texttt{fdi}, \texttt{malware}, \texttt{physical}, \texttt{insider},
\texttt{supply\_chain}, \texttt{hardware}, \texttt{other})---the vocabulary improves
materially: family presence/absence agrees in 17 of 20 for the interface family
($\kappa = 0.70$), 17 of 20 for the classical family (85\%, $\kappa = 0.32$,
prevalence-depressed), and 14 of 20 for the learned-model family ($\kappa = 0.37$; coder 2
named fewer). Table~\ref{tab:threats} and
Figure~\ref{fig:threatlife} should therefore be read at the family level; per-term counts
remain indicative of scale and ordering only. \texttt{attack\_surface\_named} is the
weakest field in the study on both statistics (0.14, PABAK 0.20, AC1 0.31). Where a field is
near-constant, $\kappa$ is undefined and we report percentage agreement instead:
\texttt{stance\_confidence}, \texttt{limitations\_stated}, \texttt{data\_available},
\texttt{ai\_llm}, \texttt{ai\_agentic} and \texttt{code\_available}.

We report these numbers because they bound the result. The headline's core count---whether a
study engages any AI-enabled threat---is coded at moderate agreement (0.60 on the collapse
that produces it), so the 35/83 figure carries real uncertainty. What this design cannot
deliver---an independent (non-author) coder, a sample of at least 50 enriched for engaged
records, and a third-party adjudicator whose tie-break does not default to coder 1---is
consolidated with the study's other design limits in Section~\ref{sec:validity} and
stated there as required work rather than as achieved. The asymmetry's \emph{direction}
is supported on every denominator and scope restriction we report; its \emph{exact
magnitude} should be quoted with the interval above.

A systematic style difference between coders emerged, and the bias indices in
Table~\ref{tab:reliability} quantify it rather than assert it: the largest between-coder
marginal differences are on \texttt{maturity} (0.35), \texttt{stance} (0.30) and
\texttt{attack\_surface\_named} (0.30), with coder~1 assigning the default category
(\emph{high}, \emph{yes}, \emph{operation}, \emph{single}) and coder~2 differentiating
(\emph{medium}, \emph{no}, \emph{multi}, \emph{unclear}) and naming more attack surfaces.
PABAK, which removes prevalence depression, lifts every field's reading---most sharply the
near-constant ones---so where $\kappa$ and PABAK disagree, the truth is that the coders
agreed often but the disagreements ran one way. We record this as a codebook problem rather
than a coder problem, and as a reason not to quote $\kappa$ alone on this sample.

\subsection{False-negative audit}

\label{sec:audit}
The strict AI-threat lexicon flagged none of the 48 records coded as not engaging, which is
consistent with the coding---but, as the methods state, the audit shares the extraction's
keyword blind spot, so this ``zero'' is weak evidence by construction. As a positive control
the same lexicon recovers only 7 of 11 known-positive studies, because some positives use generic
vocabulary the lexicon lacks (e.g.\ R17, R22, R57, R69). Recall on score-1
(mentioned-only) engagement, which covers 24 of the 35 engaged records and is the harder
case, was never measured: no score-1 positive control was run. A pattern set cannot
recognise wording it was not written for, and this audit is a pattern set applied to the
output of a pattern-assisted extraction.

We therefore also ran a broader eleven-family net. Of the 48 negatives, 38 fired on some family
and 23 fired on a strong family. All 23 strong firings were individually inspected and are
either conventional cyberattacks
discussed without any AI component or known false positives. The instructive false positives
justify human coding over keyword coding: \texttt{GaN} (the semiconductor) matching ``GAN'' in
two records (R41, R84), the author surname ``Gan'' in another (R90),
Xenon-135 reactor physics matching ``poisoning'' (R34), ARP poisoning (R68), and
the SolarWinds incident discussed as a data-poisoning example (R64). Two close calls were
examined and cleared: R86 bypasses \emph{classical} state-estimation bad-data detection,
not an ML detector, and R15 concerns a SCADA supervisor with AI detection stated only as
future work.

The 15 weak-only firings (ids 3, 7, 10, 11, 13, 18, 21, 25, 39, 45, 63, 77, 80, 88, 100)
were subsequently inspected by an author reading the released audit
windows alongside the stored abstracts---post hoc, single-reader, coded values known to the
reader, with the per-record adjudication released as \texttt{READBACK.md} in the
repository's \texttt{analysis/} directory.
Thirteen are cleared on their face: acronym lists, classical adversary prose (APT groups,
MITM, FDI, Stuxnet, BlackEnergy), defensive capability claims, related-work AI mentions,
twin-synthesised training data, and one future-work plan to use GANs to synthesise attack
data (R11), which the codebook scores as no engagement because the plan is not a
treatment. Two are borderline score-1 candidates, flagged rather than re-scored: R21's
red-team RL agents (a score-1 case only under a broad reading of agentic attack
automation, since the agents serve a defensive training loop) and R100's closing sentence
that attackers can use deep learning to enhance attacks (the single most plausible
any-level miss of the 15). If both were re-scored to 1, any-level engagement would be 37
of 83 (44.6\%); no substantive (score-2) false negative was found. A seeded 12-record
sample of the 48 negatives (R9, R10, R13, R19, R48, R62, R65, R79, R81, R85, R89, R93)
was re-read the same way: all 12 confirmed at zero, the typical clearance being classical
FDI/MITM/DoS prose, incident history, or defensive claims, and two records (R79, R89)
having fired no family at all in the full-text scan. No codes were changed by either
re-reading.

What this audit licenses is therefore narrow: the false-negative channel is disclosed
and partially inspected, not bounded. The re-readings are author-conducted and
abstract-plus-window level, so they
cannot replace the design that would bound the channel---an independent full-text re-read
of a random sample of the 48 negatives by a coder outside the author team, with a
miss-rate estimate and confidence interval propagated to the 35/83 figure. That re-read
remains feasible for the authors (the full texts are in hand) and is stated here as the
required next test; it cannot be reconstructed from the released keyword digests alone,
because the full texts are not redistributable with the package.

\subsection{Adjudication outcome}

Adjudication resolved 149 field-level disagreements and changed 20 net cells from coder~1's
values: \texttt{ml\_family} (12), \texttt{threat\_models\_named} (2), \texttt{stance} (2),
\texttt{eval\_method} (2), \texttt{dt\_type} (1) and \texttt{grid\_domain} (1). Three substantive
corrections came out of it: two studies move to \emph{bridge} stance (R22, R49), and one
study is a substation-domain study (R62), contrary to its initial coding. Two third-value
resolutions occurred, both \texttt{eval\_method} $=$ hardware-in-the-loop (R55, R62).
No headline field moved. Under the coder-1-default tie-break this is the expected outcome
(Section~\ref{sec:method}), so it should not be read as evidence that the headline is robust
to adjudication; the honest statement is that adjudication, in this design, is not a test of
the headline at all.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The asymmetry, quantified}

The corpus uses machine learning in 70 of 83 full-text studies---for detection in 55---and
engages AI-enabled threats in 35, substantively in 11; within the studies most obliged to
model attacks on their own detectors (security-primary, non-review, detection-ML, $n=29$)
the figures are 16 any (55\%, a majority) and 6 substantive (21\%). The central finding is
therefore \emph{not} that attack-side engagement is absent or even rare at the any level;
it is that \emph{any-level engagement is common in this corpus while depth is not reliably
measured by this instrument}: the substantive tier is minority on every denominator, on
the study's weakest collapse, on a threshold that partly proxies document length, and
under a misclassification interval that includes both minority and majority readings of
the any-level share. Its stability rests on the denominator and
scope restrictions of Section~\ref{sec:ai-threats} and on the misclassification
sensitivity of Section~\ref{sec:agreement}, which re-slice the same coded data and
therefore show stability rather than independent replication; the adjudication and the
keyword audit can test nothing here (Sections~\ref{sec:audit}
and~\ref{sec:agreement}). Read
within this corpus---and only this corpus, retrieved by defensive vocabulary---the
defensive side has built a substantial
apparatus of learned detectors, while the studies building it rarely examine those
detectors' own failure modes substantively. We state the finding as an asymmetry of depth,
not of rate: no temporal claim is supported by a ranked, non-random corpus, and there
is no external baseline for how deep engagement should be. One alternative reading
cannot be excluded: that shallow engagement partly reflects the corpus's paper quality
(Section~\ref{sec:validity}) rather than the field---though the cited subset engages
\emph{less} than the uncited one, which softens that concern without removing it.

\subsection{The twin as a target: strict count zero, loose tiers two-thirds}\label{sec:targettiers}

No grid study in this corpus treats the twin as the adversary's objective; the single strict
\emph{target} record (R59) concerns vehicle twins and falls outside the grid domain.
This null is presented as a descriptive observation of the coding, not as a finding about
the field, for three reasons. First, the
codebook's strict rule makes a zero the expected result in a security setting (an asset
that needs protecting is an asset an adversary may attack), and the \emph{tool} category
is a near-constant default (75 of 83). Second, the corpus's own nuclear-domain records
demonstrate that the strict categories are attainable and
attained outside the grid frame: R91's attack scenarios against the twin's digital and
communication sections (exfiltration and offline replication of a cloud twin; deception
of the twin's autonomous control; 5G jamming that starves the twin) and R92's constructed
hyperparameter attacks on the twin's ML models both read as \emph{both}-role records
(Section~\ref{sec:method} on extraction). Third, the corpus contains undeveloped
twin-as-object one-liners (R48, R88) that the strict rule deliberately sets aside. The
honest statement is domain-scoped: within this grid-domain corpus, the twin is never the
adversary's objective; in the corpus's own nuclear-domain records, it is---and the grid
difference is a finding to explain, not a universal (Section~\ref{sec:agenda}, P1).
Habeeb et al.\ name the twin as an expanded attack surface
\cite{habeeb2026}; on the loose tiers of Table~\ref{tab:twintiers}, 56 of 83 full-text
studies name an interface or model threat bearing on the twin's data path or learned
components---largely
classical grid-communication attacks that a twin-based defender would name in any case---and
51 of the 75 \emph{tool} records do so while treating the twin as an asset under protection.
The honest summary is therefore not that attack-surface engagement is absent, but that it
is \emph{defensive almost everywhere in the grid corpus}: the twin's surfaces are named as
things to protect,
and in no grid study is the twin itself the object of the attack. What we cannot speak to
is the broader version of their axis---studies in which the twin's expanded surface is the
analytical centre---because our coding separates objective from asset, not centre from
periphery.

\subsection{The lifecycle concentration, read carefully}

A descriptive result with real reach, if it holds, is the lifecycle concentration:
66 of 83 studies are coded at the operation phase, one at design---and that one (R97) is
outside the grid domain---none at commissioning or maintenance. Two
caveats apply. First, the field carries moderate agreement ($\kappa = 0.32$, PABAK 0.59,
AC1 0.54; the study's weakest field is \texttt{attack\_surface\_named}, $\kappa = 0.14$), and
coder 1, who coded 80\% of the corpus, defaults to `operation'; the 12 studies coded `multi'
may contain design or commissioning activity that the single label suppresses. Second, the score
records the phase a study 
\emph{targets}, not the phases it necessarily 
\emph{touches}. Read conservatively, the finding is therefore that the literature is dominated
by studies of running twins, with almost no study that sets out to secure a twin before it is
built, not that no design-phase security work exists. We present this as a descriptive
observation on a moderate-agreement field, not as a headline result.

\subsection{Why reviews are where the threats are named}

The two deepest engagements are reviews (R16, R50); 5 of the 11 substantive 
studies (45\%) are reviews against 34 of 83 (41\%) in the full-text corpus, so review 
authorship is not over-represented, but bridge studies are (3 of 11 against 8 of 83 full-text records, 2 more in the abstract-only set).
The honest reading is that reviews have the length and the licence to survey AI threats, while
primary papers have neither. Document length is a clear confound at both ends: it inflates
the substantive tier's accessibility for long formats---0 of 14 conference papers clear the
threshold, against 9 of 62 journal articles, 1 of 4 preprints and the single thesis
(Section~\ref{sec:method})---and these numbers should not be read as evidence that ``only
reviewers care''.

\subsection{Positioning against the prior review}

Relative to Habeeb et al.\ \cite{habeeb2026}, our contribution is a grid-side probe of a
narrower instance of one of their axes, with a published corpus, a closed vocabulary, and
reported reliability; full scope alignment is in Section~\ref{sec:targettiers}. Our
closed vocabulary covers all five of their vectors (the mapping is released as a
repository artifact and is not presented as evidence; Section~\ref{sec:related}), so the
comparison is not competing but additive, and the same holds for the wider family of
prior reviews (Table~\ref{tab:priorreviews}). Two caveats are structural, not rhetorical. First,
their search string has no specific attack-side operator, so records enter only through
security terms; the same is true of our twelve core queries, and our four adversarial-AI
probes were added precisely because of that shared blind spot. The probes cannot be
claimed as a completed defence, however: the released record does not establish that
probe-only records entered the retained corpus (Section~\ref{sec:method}), so the shallow
engagement we measure may be partly a retrieval artefact of the core vocabulary, and the
asymmetry could be weaker in the un-retrieved attack-side literature than in this corpus.
Resolving that requires releasing per-record query provenance and re-running the headline on
the probe-only subset---the concrete step recorded in the repository's provenance manifest.
Second, their corpus spans manufacturing, healthcare, water and smart city, where
the digital twin's role differs from the grid's bidirectional, safety-critical loop; grid
conclusions should not be generalised from it, nor it from us.

\subsection{Operational reading}

For operators, three interpretive notes follow (these are author readings, not coded evidence).
First, a twin whose learner is trained on the same telemetry it is meant to defend has no
independent ground truth and is a single point of failure that the corpus rarely names.
Second, runtime detection at the operation phase is necessary, but cannot cover design-stage
trust failures. Third, the near-total absence of released code and data means a twin
procurement decision cannot currently be informed by reproducible attack evidence.

\section{Research agenda}\label{sec:agenda}

We state five items. Each is tied to the gap that justifies it (from Section~\ref{sec:results}),
to the validation that would confirm it, and to the evidence that would \emph{disconfirm} it,
so the agenda is falsifiable rather than aspirational. One caveat applies to every
disconfirmation of the form ``primary studies already do this'': because the corpus is a
bounded, ranked sample with a documented retrieval gap (Section~\ref{sec:validity}, Search
bias), such studies may be found by better retrieval as easily as by new science. Finding
them would simultaneously disconfirm the gap-as-measured-in-this-corpus and narrow the
distance between the corpus and the literature; the disconfirmation conditions are stated
against the wider literature, not only against this corpus.
Table~\ref{tab:agenda} summarises.

\paragraph{P1 --- Twin-directed attack analysis for grids.}
  Build the grid-domain twin-directed attack analysis that the nuclear domain already has.
  Justified by the tiered result (strict \texttt{target} $= 0$ grid studies against 56/83
  naming interface or model threats in a defender frame, Table~\ref{tab:twintiers})
  \emph{and} by the corpus's own counterexamples: R91 and R92 already do, for nuclear
  reactors, what no grid study in this corpus does---attack scenarios against the twin
  itself (R91: exfiltration and offline replication of a cloud twin; deception of the
  twin's autonomous control) and constructed attacks on the twin's ML models (R92:
  multi-rate hyperparameter attacks). The item is therefore a transfer question, not a
  first-contact claim: grid research should either import that analysis or state why it
  does not transfer. It may not transfer for real reasons---reactor twins run
  higher-autonomy control loops under regulator-mandated cyber-informed engineering,
  while grid twins face heterogeneous legacy fleets, market-coupled timescales and
  different trust boundaries---and articulating those conditions is part of the item.
  Confirmed by a reproducible attack against a grid twin in which the twin (not the
  plant) is the objective, with released code and a stated adaptive adversary;
  disconfirmed by primary grid studies that already construct such attacks (which would
  move the strict grid count and end this gap).
\paragraph{P2 --- Measure lifecycle coverage, then extend it.}
Extend coverage from operation to design and commissioning. Justified by
  operation $=66$, design $=1$ (out-of-domain), commissioning $=0$, on a field
  with $\kappa = 0.32$ (PABAK 0.59, AC1 0.54)---one of the review's weaker fields, so the gap
  is a coding observation first and a field observation only provisionally; confirmed by
  twins whose trust boundaries, interface contracts and fail-safe
  behaviour are specified and tested before deployment, and by a double-coded re-measurement
  that survives the lifecycle field's reliability problem; disconfirmed by a re-measurement
  showing the concentration to be a coding artefact of the single-label default.
\paragraph{P3 --- Explicit adaptive threat models for the twin's learned components.}
Justified by an explicit threat model in 19/83 and an adaptive adversary in 10/83 against
  detection-ML use in 55/83; confirmed by studies that state the attacker's knowledge,
  capability and objective \emph{relative to the twin's detectors and forecasters} (e.g.\ an
  attacker who retrains against the twin's anomaly detector or probes its feedback loop) and
  test a reactive attacker; disconfirmed by primary studies that already state and test such
  models, which the corpus currently lacks.
\paragraph{P4 --- Defend the learned components, not just the plant.}
Justified by detection-dominant ML use (55/83) against shallow poisoning and evasion
  engagement (19 and 9 at any level, 7 and 1 substantive); confirmed by poisoning- and
  evasion-robust twins under distribution shift; disconfirmed by primary studies demonstrating
  robust learned detectors whose robustness is evaluated against a reactive attacker.
\paragraph{P5 --- Release the learned models, training data and attack generators.}
Not just simulation scripts. Justified by code in 3/83 (3/49 non-review) and data in
  16/83 against a corpus whose attack-side claims cannot be re-run by third parties;
  confirmed by a third party rerunning a published twin's attack evaluation unaided;
  disconfirmed by the corpus remaining release-light while attack-side claims multiply.
  This item is AI-specific in its object: what must be released are the learned models,
  their training data, and the attack generators evaluated against them---artifacts whose
  absence makes adversarial claims unverifiable in a way that releasing only simulation
  scripts would not fix.

\paragraph{Dual use and responsible disclosure.} P1 and P5, taken together, call for
building twin-directed attacks and releasing attack generators. That is deliberately
dual-use: an attack generator against twin ML models is an offensive capability in the
wrong hands, and infrastructure-security venues are right to expect the obligations to be
stated where the calls are made. Work under this agenda should follow the
responsible-disclosure norms of the community: evaluate against emulators, testbeds and
synthetic twins rather than operational assets; coordinate any vulnerability findings with
the asset owner and, where relevant, the vendor and the relevant CERT before publication;
and release attack code with usage constraints and defender-oriented documentation, so
that third parties can reproduce defensive evaluations without lowering the barrier for
unsophisticated attackers. These constraints are stated here because the agenda's
falsifiability depends on exactly the artifacts---attack generators, released
models---that carry the dual-use risk.

P5 must be stated carefully. We measure \emph{research attention}, not \emph{deployment security}.
A low attack-side count does not mean grids are undefended; it means the published research has
not modelled these attacks. We cannot estimate either the probability of an AI-enabled attack or
its expected impact from this corpus, and we do not.

\section{Threats to validity}\label{sec:validity}

\subsection{Construct validity}
``AI-enabled threat'' is a construct with two distinct directions lumped together: attacks
\emph{on} AI components and attacks \emph{wielded with} AI. The coding does not separate them
per record, so the headline 35/83 is an engagement figure, not an attack-type figure
(Section~\ref{sec:ai-threats} reports the notes-based split for the substantive studies). The
false-negative audit shows a strict lexicon misses
some positives (7/11 recovery), and shares the extraction's keyword blind spot, so it
discloses rather than bounds the false-negative channel. We mitigated by human coding with
context, by the broad eleven-family net, and by the tiered \texttt{twin\_role} reporting, but
the construct remains softer than the threat-vocabulary fields.

\subsection{Internal validity and screening}
Screening on title and abstract is imperfect, and the released record cannot quantify the
loss between abstract-level and full-text reading: no per-record artifact of the
abstract-level screening survives, so the gap is disclosed rather than measured. The
17 abstract-only records are listed with DOIs (Appendix~A) so readers can see exactly
where full-text coding was impossible.

\subsection{Reliability}
Agreement on the headline fields is moderate, not strong: the any-engagement collapse that
produces 35/83 is $\kappa = 0.60$ (bootstrap 95\% CI 0.20--0.90; the three-class $\kappa$ is
0.41); \texttt{attack\_surface\_named} is 0.14 (PABAK 0.20). Both coders are authors of this
review, the sample is 20 of 83 full-text records, and coder-1 precedence in adjudication is a
declared limitation that makes adjudication uninformative about the headline. The exact
magnitude of the asymmetry should therefore be read with the misclassification sensitivity
(36 of 83, 95\% CI 21--51), not in isolation.

\subsection{External validity and coverage}
This is not a PRISMA-systematic review: one database, original run dates not preserved (the
only dated search artifact is the 2026-10-19 reconstructed-string snapshot of
Section~\ref{sec:method}), no registered protocol, a ranked (not random) retained subset, and
five codebook revisions after the protocol was `fixed'---all of which limit how strongly the
review answers its hypothesis.
Because the corpus is a ranked subset, no prevalence figure here estimates the wider
literature, and no sampling-theory interval (Wilson or otherwise) is reported; the
misclassification sensitivity of Section~\ref{sec:agreement} is the only interval we attach
to the headline.

Seventeen of the 100 records were available only as abstracts; this is an access limitation
of the authors, not a property of the literature, and those records are listed with DOIs
(Appendix~A) so readers can check what is missing. The 17 are not a random subset: an
abstract-level reading codes 15 as \emph{tool} and two (R91, R92, both nuclear-domain
OSTI/INL reports) as twin-directed attack work; both were read (R91 in full text, R92 from its extended
abstract) and both attack twin interfaces and
twin ML models (Section~\ref{sec:method}), so the strict-target null carries its
``grid-domain'' qualifier on read evidence, not just on abstract labels. One
corpus DOI does not resolve (R18, which lies within the reliability sample) and three
records have no DOI; these are disclosed rather than dropped. Venue labels disagreed with
Crossref in 18 of 54 cases and were corrected in favour of the registry. The broad grid term
admits adjacent domains; the strict-scope sensitivity (Section~\ref{sec:ai-threats}) shows the
headline moves by at most about one and a half percentage points under the strictest reading.

\subsection{Search bias}
The core grid-security vocabulary does not retrieve attack-side work; the four adversarial-AI
probes were added to widen the candidate pool (approximately 2,000 records), but the released
record does not establish that probe-only records entered the screened set or the final 100,
and the query strings and per-record provenance were not preserved in the released package.
The probes therefore provide no retrieval-bias defence: on the released evidence, the
corpus derives from the core grid-security vocabulary alone---precisely the retrieval bias
the probes were meant to fix---and the asymmetry may be overstated in the un-retrieved
attack-side literature. The
repository's provenance manifest (`SEARCH\_PROVENANCE.md') states the artifacts whose release
would resolve this (query strings, run dates, per-record provenance, and a re-run of the
headline on the probe-only subset); until then, the corpus should be read as a bounded
sample, not a census. The three ranking components pull in opposite directions, and which
dominated the realised cut is not recoverable from the released record
(Section~\ref{sec:method}); the comparison that would establish it---the year distribution
of the 492 eligible records against the retained 100---requires the candidate-pool export
listed in the provenance manifest. What is established is the corpus's own profile: only
16 of 100 records are from 2020--2023, so foundational attack-side work from those years is
the most plausibly under-represented segment in this corpus, whatever the rule that
produced it. A dated snapshot with reconstructed query strings and a second-database
(Crossref) run has been archived (`analysis/search\_snapshot\_2026-10-19/'); the original
strings and per-record provenance remain the required artifacts. OpenAlex re-runs were
attempted twice on 2026-10-19 and blocked both times by the
shared-IP budget; re-running the search with logged queries, and ideally a second
database, is a prerequisite for any attack-side claim---not future polish---and the
provenance manifest records it as such.

\subsection{Researcher degrees of freedom}
Five codebook revisions during extraction are a real degree of freedom. They are logged, dated,
and applied consistently to all 100 records, and adjudication used the same rules; but a reader
who distrusts post-hoc refinement should weight the raw pre-adjudication codes, which are
released frozen alongside the adjudicated matrix.

\subsection{Quality appraisal, venue quality and scope}
One retained record (R69) carries unattributed authorship in a low-tier venue (IRJMETS),
lies outside the strict grid scope, and is simultaneously one of the eleven substantive
studies and one of the four AI-attack-generation exemplars---a combination that is exactly
why the venue sensitivity and DOI verification passes were necessary, and why R69 is
flagged at every use: excluding it alone moves the substantive count to 10 of 82 (5 of 28
in the core subset) without changing any direction (Section~\ref{sec:ai-threats}).
The corpus contains 67 journal articles, 17 conference
papers, 5 theses, 4 preprints, 3
reports and 4 book chapters, drawn from venues of varying
strictness (including IRJMETS and
GSC Online Press, and two Zenodo deposits classified as journal articles); 40 of the 100
had zero citations at retrieval and 36 of 100 carry the \emph{review} stance. No formal
risk-of-bias or venue-quality appraisal was performed; the venue-type filter is not a
peer-review certification, which is why Section~\ref{sec:evidence} reports a stricter
peer-review-filtered sensitivity (58 records, excluding the Zenodo deposits, the GSC
Online Press title and the IRJMETS title) alongside the full-corpus figures, and why
readers should weight venue quality into any claim reproduced from this review. The
honest consequence is that shallow engagement may partly reflect corpus quality rather
than the field: the corpus is recency-heavy, citation-light and review-heavy, and while
the cited subset actually engages \emph{less} than the uncited one
(Section~\ref{sec:ai-threats})---which softens the concern without removing it---no
available filter cleanly separates paper quality from field behaviour. The claim in the
introduction is restricted to
the intersection of `digital twin' and `cybersecurity' in power grids; substantial
adversarial-ML work on grid state estimation lies outside that intersection---the
false-data-injection and adversarial-ML literatures are large without ever naming a twin
\cite{liu2011,liang2017,biggio2018}---and is not covered
by the headline numbers.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Read as an exploratory, corpus-level measurement, the review's defensible finding is that
\emph{any-level} AI-threat engagement in this grid digital-twin security corpus is common,
while \emph{depth} is not reliably measured by the instrument used here. Across a ranked
corpus of 100 studies (a declared non-probabilistic subset of 492 eligible records, so
every figure describes the corpus, not the literature; its 76\% share of 2025--26 records
is partly a selection artefact, and its retrieval vocabulary was defensive), machine
learning is used in 70 of 83 full-text studies---for detection in 55---while 35 engage
any AI-enabled threat (a majority, 16/29 and 30/55, in the core detection-ML subsets), 11
substantively---on the weakest coder collapse, under a threshold that partly proxies
document length, and with a misclassification interval (21--51 of 83) that includes both
minority and majority readings of the any-level share. The asymmetry the review set out
to test was, in its original form, not supported; what survives is the narrower statement
above, and the title reflects it. As coded---and this is a
descriptive observation of the coding, not a finding about the field---no grid study
treats the twin as the adversary's objective; 56 of 83 name classical
interface or model threats bearing on
the twin's data path or learned components, almost
always as something to protect; and two read
nuclear-domain records (R91, R92) that do attack twin interfaces and twin ML models bound
that null from outside the grid domain, turning P1 into a transfer question rather than a
first-contact claim. Coverage, as coded, is dominated by the operation phase (66 of 83;
lifecycle $\kappa = 0.32$, AC1 0.54); the single design-phase record is outside the grid domain,
none are coded at commissioning or maintenance, and threat models are thin (explicit in
19/83).

These directions are robust to the denominator and scope restrictions we report (including a
strict grid scope and a peer-review venue filter)---which are re-slices of the same coded
data and therefore show stability, not independent replication---and to a misclassification
sensitivity anchored on the second coder (36 of 83, 95\% CI 21--51), whose anchoring is
circular and whose interval spans minority and majority readings. They are not tested by
the adjudication, whose tie-break defaults to the primary coder; the false-negative
channel is partially inspected (15 weak audit firings read; a seeded 12-record sample
of the negatives re-read; no substantive false negative found), though not bounded.
The closest prior
review names the dual role; this review measures only the narrowest, strict-objective instance
of its attack-surface axis at grid scale and finds that instance empty in the grid domain,
while the looser tiers
show the twin's surfaces widely engaged but almost always defensively, and the corpus's own
nuclear-domain records show the strict categories attainable. The broader attack-surface
axis remains untested here. The five-item agenda that follows is deliberately falsifiable:
each item names the gap that justifies it, the evidence that would close it, and the
evidence that would show the gap was never real---noting that ``already being done'' can be
revealed by better retrieval as easily as by new science, and that the dual-use obligations
of twin-directed attack work are stated alongside it.
